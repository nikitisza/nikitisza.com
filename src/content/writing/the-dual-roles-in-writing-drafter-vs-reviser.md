I recently finished [Jessica Brody](https://www.jessicabrody.com/)’s [course on writer’s block](https://www.linkedin.com/learning/conquering-writer-s-block), in which she teaches us the <strong><em>importance of wearing 2 hats during the writing process.</em></strong>

I don’t know about you, but as a non-native English writer, I often struggle with self-doubt about the quality of my writing and want to <strong>*not only finish my article*</strong> within a certain timeframe <strong><em>but also create a valuable and exceptional piece.</em></strong>

Jessica says that writing involves <strong>2 distinctive roles — the Drafter and the Reviser.</strong>

The desire to reduce my article writing time from <strong>*5–6 hours*</strong> to half had been on my mind for a long time. As a result of applying the lessons she taught me to my writing, I can write a piece in a maximum of <strong>*3 hours*</strong>.

I wanted to write so well in the past that it inhibited me from completing an article. There was a time when it took <strong>*weeks to write a piece*</strong> because I was going back and forth to the same sentence during my draft phase. I couldn’t let a sentence go without perfecting it.

<strong>It took so much time!</strong>

I spent hours only on the introduction. No wonder why I couldn’t publish more often.

<em>In product development, we always say,</em> <strong><em>‘Let’s ship it!’</em></strong> <em>in other words:</em>

Done is better than perfect!

We can easily apply this sentiment to writing.

Now, in October 2023, I challenged myself to submit an article every day. So far, I kept up with my self-imposed goal. <strong><em>Here’s how.</em></strong>

# The Role of the Drafter

There’s no other role of a drafter than to <strong>*create a preliminary version*</strong> of an article or chapter in a book.

You have a couple of **main responsibilities** when you wear the Drafter hat.

1.  Generating and executing ideas
2.  Experimenting and drafting without obsessing over perfection

During this phase, **you’re not responsible for**

-   Perfecting word choice
-   Writing intricate descriptions
-   Re-writing large sections
-   Fixing typos

<strong>The output of the Drafter?</strong>

<em>An ugly & messy draft.</em> And that’s how it should be.

# The Role of the Reviser

After the draft is complete, you put the Reviser hat on and are ready to refine and beautify the article or chapter.

Jessica uses the <strong><em>analogy of the house.</em></strong>

Think about it as the <em>Drafter builds the house; the Reviser decorates it.</em>

It’s important *not to revise an unfinished draft* because we can’t enhance an article that doesn’t exist yet.

# Why It’s Crucial to Separate the Two Roles

<strong><em>Merging the roles leads to stagnation.</em></strong>

Many writers attempt to perfect while they draft, causing unnecessary stalls and stress. I was part of this group until recently, when I changed my approach.

The key is to **embrace the ‘Crappy Writer’ hat**, focus solely on progress, and ignore quality.

Don’t be distracted by word choices, punctuation, or sentence fragments. That’s the problem for the ‘Fancy Writer,’ not the ‘Crappy Writer.’ The <strong>‘Fancy Writer’ will elevate your work</strong>; trust that!

# Key Message

I took this advice seriously because Jessica Brody has published *over 20 books* in her career, so I believe she can be trusted.

<strong>We don’t find many writers consistent enough to be worth copying.</strong>

I only take advice from people <em>who actually do the work.</em> You shouldn’t take my advice; you should take <strong>*Jessica’s advice*</strong>.

There might be another approach that I haven’t tried yet, but I did try many, and this is the <strong>*only one that works for me*</strong> so far, so I keep running with it because it’s been transformational to my writing journey, like nothing else.

Happy drafting and revising!
