When I worked for startups, I wished I had these tips. Instead, I was trying to produce verbose sentences that were grammatically correct.

I understand the unique challenges when working as a solo, team-of-one designer and wearing multiple hats — including content design.

Despite no longer working for startups and having dedicated content designers on my team, I still visit these tips from time to time.

### **Tip #1 — Keep it simple**

<strong>OK:</strong> “The coloration of the clickable button is blue.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “The button is blue.”

<strong>Why:</strong> A good sentence uses simple language that is easy to understand.

### Tip #2 — **Use active voice**

<strong>OK:</strong> “Your account has been deleted by our system.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “We deleted your account.”

<strong>Why:</strong> Active voice is direct and easy to understand, which improves clarity.

### Tip #3 — **Avoid jargon**

<strong>OK:</strong> “The JSON file was corrupted.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “There was a problem with the file.”

<strong>Why:</strong> Not all users will understand technical terms. It’s best to keep the language clear and simple.

### Tip #4 — **Be concise**

<strong>OK:</strong> “In order to proceed with the process, you are required to press the button marked as ‘Continue’.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “Press ‘Continue’ to proceed.”

<strong>Why:</strong> Shorter, to-the-point sentences reduce cognitive load and increase understanding.

### Tip #5 — **Use personal language**

<strong>OK:</strong> “Users should update their profiles.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “Update your profile.”

<strong>Why:</strong> Speaking directly to the user creates a more engaging and personal experience.

### Tip #6 — **Align with the brand tone**

<strong>OK:</strong> “Hey there! Wanna see your account details?” (for a professional business application)

<strong>Better:</strong> “View your account details.”

<strong>Why:</strong> The writing should match the brand’s tone to provide a consistent user experience.

### Tip #7 — **Provide clear error messages**

<strong>Not great:</strong> “Error 404.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “We can’t find the page you’re looking for.”

<strong>Why:</strong> Clear error messages help users understand what went wrong and what they can do next. Add an example, “Did you mean xyz page”?

### Tip #8 — **Use positive language**

<strong>OK:</strong> “Don’t forget to save your work.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “Remember to save your work.”

<strong>Why:</strong> Positive language is more engaging and user-friendly.

### Tip #9 — **Avoid double negatives**

<strong>OK:</strong> “Do not uncheck this box if you don’t want to avoid unsubscribing.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “Check this box to keep your subscription.”

<strong>Why:</strong> Double negatives can confuse users, making it harder to understand the action.

### Tip #10 — **Prioritize readability**

<strong>Not great:</strong> “Savechanges” or “SAVE ALL CHANGES” or “Save Changes.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “Save changes”

<strong>Why:</strong> Proper spacing and capitalization improve readability and comprehension.

### Tip #11 — **Give clear instructions**

<strong>OK:</strong> “Go ahead.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “Click ‘Next’ to continue.”

<strong>Why:</strong> Specific instructions help guide the user’s actions.

### Tip #12 — **Use numerals for numbers**

<strong>OK:</strong> “Your package will arrive in two days.” and “You have thirty minutes to activate your account.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “Your package will arrive in 2 days.” and “You have 30 minutes to activate your account.”

<strong>Why:</strong> Numerals stand out better and are easier to scan than spelled-out numbers. Also, think about all the non-native speakers who might use the product.

### Tip #13 — **Use verbs to start action button labels**

<strong>OK:</strong> “Agreement acceptance”

<strong>Better:</strong> “Accept agreement”

<strong>Why:</strong> Action words at the beginning of button labels make it clear what will happen when the user clicks.

### Tip #14 — **Use descriptive link text**

<strong>OK:</strong> “Click here.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “Learn more about our privacy policy.”

<strong>Why:</strong> Descriptive link text improves accessibility and helps set appropriate expectations about what users will find when they click.

### Tip #15 — **Write for your audience**

<strong>Not great:</strong> “Please submit your query.” (for a children’s app)

<strong>Better:</strong> “Ask us anything!”

<strong>Why:</strong> Knowing your audience helps you write in a way that best communicates with them.

### Tip #16 — **Use consistent terminology**

<strong>OK:</strong> Using “Cart”, “Basket”, and “Bag” interchangeably on an e-commerce site.

<strong>Better:</strong> Consistently using “Cart” throughout the site.

<strong>Why:</strong> Consistent terminology enhances clarity and prevents confusion.

### Tip #17 — **Explain acronyms and abbreviations**

<strong>OK:</strong> “Our CDN improves site performance.”

Better: “Our Content Delivery Network (CDN) improves site performance.”

Why: Not all users will be familiar with specific acronyms or abbreviations, so it’s best to explain them.

### Tip #18 — **Provide context for data**

OK: “You’ve earned 1500 points.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “You earned 1500 points — enough for a $15 voucher.”

<strong>Why:</strong> Providing context helps users understand the relevance or value of the information.

### Tip #19 — **Communicate time frames accurately**

<strong>OK:</strong> “We’ll get back to you soon.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “We’ll respond within 24 hours.”

<strong>Why:</strong> Providing specific time frames sets realistic expectations for the user.

### **Tip #20 — Use simple verb tense**

<strong>OK:</strong> “The money has been sent.”

<strong>Better:</strong> “Money sent.”

<strong>Why:</strong> Using simple verb tense (e.g. past simple) makes sentences easier to comprehend.

### Tip #21 — Test your content

<strong>OK:</strong> “Continue with the task.”

<strong>Better:</strong> After testing, you may find that “Keep going” performs better.

<strong>Why:</strong> Testing different phrases helps identify which best drives user engagement.
