Here’s what I’ve seen in the past, <strong><em>constantly.</em></strong>

> A team runs user interviews after they’ve already designed the solution.

> They ask <strong>leading questions.</strong>

> They cherry-pick quotes that support their direction.

> They **ignore feedback** that contradicts the roadmap.

> That’s not research.

That’s confirmation bias with extra steps.

Look at these temperature graphs.

<figure class="w-richtext-figure-type-image"><div><img alt="" src="/images/writing/your-user-research-isnt-unbiased/69c32f079437c35f06697c44_1-ve2qzbzofchujntuyg0rwg.jpeg" loading="lazy"></div><figcaption>Reference: The Pocket — Universal Principles of Design (026)</figcaption></figure>

Same data.

<strong>Wildly different stories depending on what you want to believe.</strong>

Your user research does the same thing.

> You believe your design solves the problem.

> So you unconsciously ask questions that confirm it.

> You interpret ambiguous feedback as supportive.

> You dismiss contradicting evidence as edge cases.

<strong>Real research kills your assumptions.</strong>

> Uncomfortable research changes your direction.

Next time you run research, ask yourself: <strong>when’s the last time user feedback completely stopped a project?</strong>

If you can’t remember, you’re not doing research. You’re doing confirmation bias with a research budget.
